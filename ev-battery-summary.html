Energy Economics: EV Battery Repurposing

Third-Year Mechanical Engineering Project (2025)

Nepal’s rapid adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) is expected to generate a growing stock of batteries reaching the end of their automotive life. Although these batteries are retired from vehicle use at around 80% State of Health (SoH), they retain substantial residual capacity suitable for second-life energy storage applications. Repurposing such batteries can reduce e-waste generation, improve resource efficiency, and enhance the overall value of imported EV technologies.

This study evaluates the technical and economic feasibility of repurposing EV batteries in the context of Nepal, with a primary focus on energy economics. Fleet-level data from CATL-based LFP battery packs were analyzed to assess degradation behavior, remaining usable capacity, and second-life performance. Economic evaluation was carried out using benefit–cost ratio (BCR), break-even analysis, and payback assessment based on Nepal Electricity Authority time-of-day tariffs.

Results show that batteries retired at approximately 75–80% SoH can deliver an additional 7–10 years of service in stationary applications under controlled operating conditions. Small- and medium-scale second-life systems demonstrate economic feasibility with BCR values greater than unity. However, a 3 MWh grid-connected second-life battery system relying solely on energy arbitrage generates total revenues of approximately NPR 5.23 million over its operational life, against an initial capital cost of about NPR 10 million, resulting in a BCR below one. Inclusion of extended utilization benefits and end-of-life material recovery improves overall economic performance. The study concludes that second-life EV batteries can contribute meaningfully to Nepal’s energy transition when deployed selectively and supported by appropriate economic and policy frameworks.
